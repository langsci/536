\title{Expressing future reference in Middle Low German}
\abstract{The article deals with future constructions in Middle Low German and with the question of which external and internal linguistic factors have an influence on the choice of the formally different variants referring to future events in Middle Low German. The study is based on transcriptions and annotations of the \mbox{Reference} \mbox{Corpus} Middle Low German / Low Rhenish (1200–1650). In addition to a purely quantitative part, the article presents a deep quantitative-qualitative investigation of future constructions in Middle Low German Bible texts and reveals various factors that have an impact on the choice of a variant – above all there is an influence of the translator, as well as an influence of the semantic context.}

\section{Introduction}
Future constructions are intensively researched in Germanic languages both in their recent and in their historical varieties. However, with regard to historical varieties of the German language, there are still some research gaps, concerning for example the diachronic development of competing constructions expressing future and the role of internal and external factors influencing the use of a variant. This is especially true for Middle Low German,\footnote{Middle Low German is a group of historical varieties of Low German. It was used from 1200 until about 1650 not only in the northern German-speaking areas but also in the Baltic area and elsewhere in centres of the Hanseatic League such as London or Nowgorod, since it was the lingua franca of this confederation.} the development of which is less researched compared to the High German varieties.\footnote{The development of future constructions in High German is intensively researched with differing focus regarding content and methodological focus. A complete overview of research cannot be given here, so reference is made only to selected works. See for example \citet{Saltveit1962} analysing sentences with both \textit{werden} ('become') with the present participle and with the infinitive from Old High German until the 16\textsuperscript{th} century and \citet{Oubouzar1974} providing a quantitative and qualitative study of \textit{werden} + present participle and infinitive in a set of seven texts from the 11\textsuperscript{th} until the 17\textsuperscript{th} century.} In the old Middle Low German grammars by \citet{Lübben1882}, \citet{Lasch1914}, and \citet{Sarauw1924} (see Section \ref{chap:lit}), future constructions are only briefly referred to. While the interest in Middle Low German grammar has increased in the last few years and a wide range of grammatical issues including complex verbal constructions have been investigated, future constructions have not been in the centre of interest yet. A recent corpus-based study by \citet{Ihden2023} on \textit{wērden}/\textit{wēsen} + present participle contains some useful observations concerning the futurate use of \textit{wērden} + present participle. However, this study did not provide a comprehensive description of the variation between \textit{wērden} + present participle and \textit{wērden} + infinitive as well as the other competing future constructions as the focus of the analysis was not on future expressions.
Hence, findings on the development of future expressions in Middle Low German and on influencing factors remain to be obtained.

This key research desideratum is addressed in our paper focusing on the diachronic development of the formally different variants expressing future in Middle Low German and on interactions between them, especially on the reasons for the use of a specific variant. In order to do so, we use transcriptions and annotations from the Reference Corpus Middle Low German / Low Rhenish (1200–1650) (short: ReN) (\cite{ReNTeam2021}). The ReN is currently the largest corpus of Middle Low German texts. It is enriched with various linguistic annotations and the text selection is structured by time, language area, and genre, which allows us to analyse phenomena that vary along each parameter.

We will conduct a two-step analysis on the variation of future expressions in Middle Low German, starting with a purely quantitative approach to provide a bigger picture of frequency (Section \ref{chap:freq}) which we will complement with a quantitative-qualitative approach on variation of future constructions in Bible texts and on factors influencing the use of a variant (Section \ref{chap:bibles}).

Our paper is structured as follows: In Section \ref{chap:lit} we give a brief overview of the different future constructions in Middle Low German and on the previous research in this area. Section \ref{chap:method} contains our research questions, explains our method and presents the corpus we used. The results of our analyses are given in Section \ref{chap:results}, starting with an overview on the distribution of two formally similar variants, namely \textit{wērden} + present participle and \textit{wērden} + infinitive (Section \ref{chap:freq}), followed by a quantitative-qualitative analysis of variation in future references in Middle Low German Bible texts (Section \ref{chap:bibles}). In Section \ref{chap:summary}, we summarise the main results and give an outlook on potential following studies based on our work.

\section{Middle Low German future constructions in the research literature}\label{chap:lit}

The Middle Low German reference grammars by \citet{Lübben1882}, \citet{Lasch1914}, and \citet{Sarauw1924} describe four different grammatical structures for referring to future events. 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Modal verb constructions:
    \subitem a) \textit{schȫlen} (‘shall’) + infinitive: \textit{hê schal kōmen}
    \subitem b) \textit{willen}  (‘will’) + infinitive: \textit{hê wil kōmen}
    \subitem c) \textit{mö̂ten}  (‘must’) + infinitive:  \textit{mö̂t kōmen} %\textit{ö{\fontspec{Symbola}\symbol{"02C6}}} %@Lena: Graphem!
    \item Present tense:
    \subitem d) Main verb in simple present: \textit{hê kōmet}
    \item Complex verbal construction with \textit{wērden} (‘become’) in present indicative and a present participle:
    \subitem e) \textit{wērden} + present participle: \textit{hê wērt kōmende}
     \item Complex verbal construction with \textit{wērden} (‘become’) in present indicative and an infinitive:
    \subitem f) \textit{wērden} + infinitive: \textit{hê wērt kōmen} 
\end{itemize}

According to \citet[222--223, § 412, note 6]{Lasch1914}, the most common choices, especially in earlier texts and in texts from the western areas, are modal constructions with the verbs \textit{schȫlen} (‘shall’) and \textit{willen}  (‘will’) as in \textit{hê schal kōmen} in a) or \textit{hê wil kōmen} in b) meaning `he will come'. Sometimes the modal verb \textit{mö̂ten} %@Lena: Graphem!
(‘must’) is used in that sense as well, as in \textit{hê mö̂t kōmen} in c).\footnote{The forms of the lexemes given here are based on the Middle Low German dictionary \citep{MNWB} which differentiates between old length (e.g. \textit{ê}) and new length (e.g. \textit{ē}).} \citet[91, § 65]{Lübben1882} states that no differences in the use of these three variants could be detected~-- as an illustration, he cites a Bible translation by a chronicler from Bremen containing both \textit{mö̂ten} %  %@Lena: Graphem!
+ infinitive and \textit{willen}  + infinitive within the same sentence (\textit{ik mot voruolgen myne vyende vnde ik wil nicht vmmekeren} `I will follow my enemies and I will not turn back'). \citet[225]{Sarauw1924} considers the present as in \textit{hê kōmet} in d) the primary and mainly used option for expressing future. Aside from the present tense and modal verbs + infinitive, there are also two complex verbal constructions with \textit{wērden} (‘become’) in present indicative conveying a future meaning in Middle Low German, namely \textit{wērden} + present participle as in \textit{hê wērt kōmende} in e) and \textit{wērden} + infinitive as in \textit{hê wērt kōmen} in f). 
According to \citet[91, § 65]{Lübben1882}, a form such as \textit{kōmen} in f) must not be interpreted as an infinitive but as a present participle which has lost its ending -\textit{de}. Through time, this new form without the -\textit{de} becomes common for expressing a future event. 
It is described by \citet[92, § 65]{Lübben1882} as typical for High German varieties where it already spread from the 14\textsuperscript{th} century on.
The formal ambiguity between the present participle and the infinitive is also mentioned by \citet[222--223, § 412, note 6]{Lasch1914}. She states that the infinitive becomes the dominant variant within the complex verbal constructions with \textit{wērden} expressing future events.

Information on the different variants expressing future in Middle Low German can also be found in a grammar book from the Middle Low German time itself, the so-called \textit{Münster Grammatik} (1451). According to the text, which explains tense and mood of the verb using the example of the Latin verb \textit{legēre}, the Latin form \textit{legam} (`I will read') is future indicative and corresponds to Middle Low German ``ick wil edder ick schal lesen, edder alse de averlender seggen: ik werde lesen" (`I will or I shall read, or as the foreigners (from the upper German regions, as opposed to the low lands) say: I become read' \citep[44]{Wilken1877}.\footnote{See also the new edition of the \textit{Münster Grammatik} by \citet[61--63]{Caparrini2018}.} The text thus contrasts the variants \textit{willen} + infinitive and \textit{schȫlen} + infinitive used in Middle Low German with the variant \textit{wērden} + infinitive used in High German areas. In addition, the author explains the subtle differences in the use of \textit{willen} + infinitive and \textit{schȫlen} + infinitive as future expressions. While \textit{willen} + infinitive is used when someone has the will to carry out the mentioned action, \textit{schȫlen} + infinitive is preferred when one is obliged to carry out this action \citep[44]{Wilken1877}. Thus, a semantic difference between the two constructions is established here. Furthermore, the \textit{Münster Grammatik} deals with Latin verb forms in the future imperative as in \textit{legitō} (`read!') which means that someone is obliged to read in the future: ``dat he in einer tokamender tyd lesen schal" `that he shall read in a future time' \citep[45]{Wilken1877}. Since such an obligation is connected with the variant \textit{schȫlen} + infinitive as described above, it is most likely to serve as an equivalent for the Latin future tense in the imperative.
Besides diachronic, diatopic and semantic differences between the variants, there are even more factors with an impact on the use of a variant for expressing future. An influence of the author, the scribe or the printer of a text is shown by \citet[440]{Ihden2023}, who provides a corpus-based analysis of \textit{wērden} and \textit{wēsen} + present participle with data from the ReN (see above). As \citet[440]{Ihden2023} shows, \textit{wērden} + present participle is rather frequent in Middle Low German Bible translations, where future expressions in the Latin Vulgate are translated very closely. Moreover, a cursory look at sentences with future expressions in the Bible texts from the ReN revealed that the same sentence from the Bible can be translated using formally different constructions for referring to future events (\cite[436]{Ihden2023}). Hence, the use of a variant seems to depend strongly on the person producing the text. In the case of the Middle Low German Bible texts, this is the translator.

As we have outlined, there are no comprehensive studies on the development of the different constructions expressing future in Middle Low German. Much research on future constructions has been done focusing on the older High German varieties, however. Regarding the future construction \textit{werden} + infinitive in the German language, there has been a controversial discourse on where the construction comes from for decades, and several scenarios on the origin and the development of \textit{werden} + infinitive have been worked out (also see Schnee and Hartmann, this Volume). One theory states that \textit{werden} + infinitive derived from \textit{werden} + present participle by a phonological reduction of the participle, namely the loss of the final -\textit{e} and the assimilation of the consonant cluster -\textit{nd} (Middle High German -\textit{ende} > -\textit{en)} \citep[397--398, § 373]{weinhold1967}.\footnote{\citet[397, § 373]{weinhold1967} notes with reference to \citet{bech1901} that this loss of the -\textit{e} was mainly spread in the Middle German varieties but not in Southern German varieties. In line with this, \citet[91]{Kleiner1925} shows that in Alemannic texts from the 13\textsuperscript{th} century there is no loss of the ending -\textit{de} in present participle forms. Hence, the attestations of the reduced and assimilated forms are not in line with the areal development of the \textit{werden} future. Because it extends from the Southern German speaking areas, one would have expected attestations for the loss of the participle ending if the theory was correct (\cite[3]{Jäger2024}).} A second theory, the so-called analogy theory, explains the development of \textit{werden} + infinitive by an analogy to Middle High German \textit{beginnen} or modal verbs + infinitive (e.g. \cite[177]{Wilmanns1899}).\footnote{In contrast to these mono-factorial explanations, \citet{DiewaldHabermann2005} propose a multi-factorial approach and explain the development of the \textit{werden} future as an interaction between grammaticalisation, language contact and socio-cultural factors.} See \citet{Westvik2000} for an overview of the different theories.
\largerpage[2]

Two studies, however, must be mentioned here that do include Middle Low German data. That is, for once, \citegen{kurrelmeyer1904} extensive study, which \citet{Westvik2000} counts as a contribution to the analogy theory, and \citet{Schmid2000}, which according to Westvik proposes what he calls a theory of contamination, as he suggests that the contamination of formally different constructions with a similar meaning leads to the emergence of \textit{werden} + infinitive.

\citet{kurrelmeyer1904} looks at a broad range of constructions potentially used for future reference (\textit{wollen}+ infinitive, \textit{sollen}+ infinitive, present, \textit{werden}~+ infinitive, \textit{werden} + present participle as well as \textit{werden} + past participle, \textit{werden}~+ adjective and \textit{werden} + noun) and lists the proportions each of these have in the total number of future references for a total of 209 texts, 16 of which are Middle Low German. In the latter he identifies 497 future references. Nearly 50\% of these are expressed with \textit{wollen}\footnote{\citet{kurrelmeyer1904} subsumes the Low German verbs under their High German counterparts. We adopt the author's wording in this paragraph.} + infinitive, followed by  \textit{sollen} + infinitive with about 20\%. 13\% of the future references use the present tense. \textit{werden} + infinitive makes up 11\%, however, this construction is only found in three of the texts. It has a substantial part only in one text, namely \textit{Reynke de vos} (Lübeck 1498). Less than 3\% of the instances have \textit{werden} + present participle. The rest of the future references are expressed with constructions not typically counted as future markers but do have futurate semantics to them, namely \textit{werden} with a past participle, an adjective or a noun. Overall, \citet[89]{kurrelmeyer1904} concludes for the Low German texts that ``the first person is represented chiefly by \textit{wollen} + \textit{infinitive}, the second is not sharply defined, now using one, now the other form, the third uses all three constructions, that is, \textit{present tense}, \textit{sollen} or \textit{wollen} + \textit{infinitive}" (emphasis in original). He sees a tendency of the formerly prevailing form \textit{sollen}~+ infinitive (p. 32) to be replaced by \textit{wollen} + infinitive. 

\citet{Schmid2000} looks at 100 different versions of the \textit{Fifteen Signs Before Doomsday} from the 12\textsuperscript{th} to the 17\textsuperscript{th} century. Due to the nature of its content (prophecies) and its consistency, this corpus allows for an onomasiological study, looking at formal variation. As the different versions come from different areas and centuries, it can be used for diachronic and diatopic studies.\footnote{The corpus is not balanced within area and time, however. It shows a preponderance of Upper German texts and other dialects areas are not represented either in the earlier or the later periods, compare \citet[242]{Westvik2000}.} He reports about the five Middle Low German texts that in two of these, which date from the 15\textsuperscript{th} century, the present construction is the more frequent variant, while in two texts from the 16\textsuperscript{th} century, \textit{sollen} + infinitive dominates.\footnote{The numbers presented here are unfortunately somewhat elusive: In the overview of the texts in his corpus, \citeauthor{Schmid2000} reports four Middle Low German texts from the 15\textsuperscript{th}, and only one from the 16\textsuperscript{th} Century. While the overall difference could be explained by texts that have no clear leading variant, the discrepancy in the number of texts from the 16\textsuperscript{th} century is not accounted for.}

Overall, he notes that the \textit{sollen} construction is particularly well established in Middle German, Middle Low German and Middle Dutch, while the Upper German area is characterised by the spread of the \textit{werden} construction.\footnote{\citet{Westvik2000} questions the validity of this assessment regarding the origin of the \textit{werden}-construction, based on the already mentioned imbalance of the data set. This critique does not apply to the statements about Low German, for which, however, very little data was collected.}

As large corpora for historical German varieties have been published in recent decades, the development of future constructions is increasingly researched using corpus-linguistic methods. Regarding older varieties of German, the largest amount of data comes from the Reference Corpora on German language history (\cite{Ihdenetal2023}), namely the Reference Corpora of Old German (short ReA), Middle High German (short ReM), Early New High German (short ReF) and Middle Low German / Low Rhenish (short ReN) as well as German Inscriptions (short ReDI).

\largerpage[-1]

In a recent study based on Old High German and Old Saxon data from the ReA, \citet{Jäger2024} provides an alternative scenario concerning the development of \textit{werden} + infinitive in German. According to her, the origin of the construction lies in copula constructions with \textit{werden} and a predicative past participle. \citet[189]{Jäger2024} lists a number of conditions which have to be fulfilled in order to have a bridging context where there is an ambiguity between the past participle and the infinitive. In the data from the ReA she finds nine clear occurrences of such bridging contexts; one for Old High German and eight for Old Saxon. \citet[183]{Jäger2024} points out that her scenario accounts for both the diachronic and the diatopic extension of \textit{werden} + infinitive in High German. As the prefix \textit{ge}- becomes an obligatory marker of the past participle in the 15\textsuperscript{th} and 16\textsuperscript{th} century, there is a higher chance that a speaker considers an ambiguous form without the prefix to be an infinitive (\cite[229]{Jäger2024}). This supports the reanalysis of \textit{werden}~+ past participle as \textit{werden} + infinitive. With regard to the areal distribution, \citet[230]{Jäger2024} notes that the development differs between High German and Low German. In High German, the past participle is usually formed with the prefix \textit{ge}- and the infinitive without it, while no such formal difference exists in Low German, as the past participle can still be formed without this prefix. Therefore, High German speakers tended to reanalyse a past participle without the \textit{ge}- as an infinitive, unlike speakers of Low German, who were accustomed to the formal similarity of the past participle and the infinitive.

\citet{Hartmann2021} provides an analysis of competing constructions expressing future in Middle High German and Early New High German using data from the ReM and the Bonn Early New High German Corpus (short FnhdC).\footnote{See also \citet{Luther2013} who uses a sample of texts from the ReM in order to investigate which different formal expressions are used for referring to future events in Middle High German. Unfortunately, her procedure for identifying the attestations for future reference is not completely transparent, which is reflected in the fact that she presents attestations that are not unambiguous future references.} With regard to the frequency of the different constructions, \citet[376--378]{Hartmann2021} shows that both the use of \textit{werden} + present participle and of \textit{werden} + infinitive increases during the 12\textsuperscript{th} century. But, from the 13\textsuperscript{th} century on the frequency of \textit{werden} + present participle declines while the frequency of \textit{werden} + infinitive increases even more. In contrast to the constructions with \textit{werden}, the use of the modal verbs + infinitive remains at a relatively stable level through the Middle High German and the Early New High German period.

Besides frequency, \citet{Hartmann2021} also analyses which semantic verb types (according to the aktionsart categorization of \cite{Vendler1957}) are used in the main verb slot of the different constructions. He finds a parallel development of both \textit{werden} + present participle and \textit{werden} + infinitive. First, in the 12\textsuperscript{th} century, almost only activities are used as main verbs within the two constructions. From the 13\textsuperscript{th} century on, \citet[378--379]{Hartmann2021} observes a context expansion since not only activities but also achievements and accomplishments occur in the main verb slot. \citet[379]{Hartmann2021} shows that in both constructions the frequency of telic verbs increases. In the modal verb constructions, which are used as a contrast here, the frequency of the different semantic verb types stays nearly the same through time (\cite[380--381]{Hartmann2021}). From these results \citet[382--383]{Hartmann2021} draws the conclusion that both \textit{werden} + present participle and \textit{werden} + infinitive have gone through a similar development of grammaticalisation, since they could be used in the same contexts and therefore both were competing constructions for a specific period of time. This phase of \textit{attraction} is followed by a phase of \textit{differentiation}\footnote{\citet[389--390]{Hartmann2021} uses the terms \textit{attraction} and \textit{differentiation} according to \citet{DeSmet2018}, who explain that competing forms can change their functions by undergoing two phases: In the first phase, the attraction, two constructions with specific functional similarities become even more similar (\cite[203--204]{DeSmet2018}). In the second phase, the differentiation, ``the functional domain competed over ends up being divided, with each expression filling a unique functional niche" (\cite[198]{DeSmet2018}).} concerning the semantics of the constructions. While nearly all of the few occurrences for \textit{werden} + present participle in the FnhdC carry an inchoative semantic (\cite[390]{Hartmann2021}), with \textit{werden} + infinitive, the future reading becomes stronger.

So far, no corpus-based studies have been conducted to assess the domain of future constructions in Middle Low German. With this paper we address this deficit and contribute to a more complete picture of the development of future constructions in historical varieties of German. 

\section{Research questions, methods and corpus used}\label{chap:method}
We work with a broad definition of (future) tense: Formally, this means that we include any grammatical form (i.e. morphological markers or periphrastic auxiliary constructions) that is used to express future (i.e. situate an event\footnote{We use the term \textit{event} in the broad sense to describe any type of activity, process or state \citep[85]{Ultan1978}.} posterior to a reference point, i.e. the utterance time, compare \cite[287--288]{Reichenbach1947}.) We further acknowledge that referencing to the future is rarely the sole function of a construction \citep[52]{comrie_identifying_1989}.
The  most commonly found atemporal functions of future markers are modal categories (such as imperative, obligation, necessity or polite request). Besides these, future markers can express e.g. aspectual categories (such as gnomic/general truth and habitual event), as well as some (non spatial) goal-oriented categories \citep[102--106]{Ultan1978}. Markers of modality, particularly those that express obligation, volition, uncertainty or irreality, are also the most common source from which future markers develop, followed by aspectual markers or (spatial) goal-oriented categories \citep[118]{Ultan1978}. 
The fact that modality and future tense are so closely connected is often attributed to ``the relative uncertainty inherent in both future event and most of the categories subsumed under the general heading of modals"  \citep[118]{Ultan1978}. 

It is noteworthy that this is but one of the many similarities and semantic overlaps between these categories that are `atemporal' in a stricter sense but strongly connected to the category of future tense. \citet[73, 82]{morris1990} considers them subcategories of \textit{future} (\textit{future of intent}, \textit{future of obligation} and \textit{future of ingression}), which he suggests is to be seen as a broader, complex cognitive category. We do not (yet) make any claims about whether or not expressing future tense can be said to be the primary function of any of the forms looked at. The centre of interest of this paper is to identify the factors that influence the use of the different variants used in Middle Low German in order to refer to a future event (Section \ref{chap:bibles}). 

First, Section \ref{chap:freq} gives an overview over the diachronic distribution of two different competing future expressions in Middle Low German. We will take a look at the frequencies of \textit{wērden} in the present indicative with a present participle and with an infinitive in the ReN (\cite{ReNTeam2021})  and compare them with those found by \citet{Hartmann2021} in the High German corpora. The ReN contains 235 texts from 1200 until about 1650 with a total amount of 2.3 million tokens, of which 1.5 million tokens are grammatically annotated with respect to part of speech (pos), inflectional morphology, and lemma. The remaining 800,000 tokens are transcribed without annotations.
The ReN is structured by the parameters of language area, time, and genre, which allows an examination of the potential influence of these factors on grammatical variation.

Due to their multi-functionality, the present forms as well as the modal constructions\footnote{\citet{Hartmann2021} uses sets of modal constructions as a background to compare the semantic features analysed against, in order to control that the development he observes in the \textit{werden} constructions can be considered distinctive for the latter and not merely a general development shared by all verbal phrases.} are not included in this first analysis. In order to collect all occurrences of \textit{wērden} with either an infinitive or a present participle, we searched the corpus for sentences containing the lexeme \textit{wērden} (annotated with the lemma \textit{wērden$^1$} in the ReN) as well as an infinitive or a present participle and then manually sorted out the cases where the latter is not a complement to the instance of \textit{wērden}. We verified that each instance also carries future semantics. Instances of preterital \textit{wērden} were excluded for this study, as future meaning is only expressed when the auxiliary verb is in the present tense. We also excluded instances of \textit{wērden} that are unambiguous subjunctive forms\footnote{The paradigm regularly shows syncretism of the indicative and the subjunctive in the first and second person singular as well as in the plural. Due to graphematic variation, further syncretism can arise. As the proportion of instances tagged as ambiguous is rather high, we decided to include both distinctly indicative forms and those that are not explicitly subjunctive (i.e. those tagged as ambiguous for mode).}, leaving us with a data set of 614 instances in sum (326 instances of \textit{wērden} + present participle and 288 of \textit{wērden} + infinitive).\footnote{The relevant data and scripts for both sub-studies are available in an OSF repository, which can be accessed via \href{https://osf.io/4c6yv }{https://osf.io/4c6yv}.}

The second part of our study (Section \ref{chap:bibles}) aims to cover all the formally different constructions used for future reference (including the present tense and the modal constructions not considered in the purely quantitative approach). This onomasiological perspective entails the difficulty of obtaining an adequate data set, as the entirety of instances is not formally identifyable (due to the multiple functions most of the variants can have). 
For the present study, we draw on the fact that future tense is morphologically represented in Latin. We look at three Middle Low German Bible prints that are translations of the Latin Vulgate. More specifically, we examine the way each of the texts realises those sentences that have a future form in the Latin text.
Notably, the formal identification of Latin future instances will not only yield such attestations with true futurate meaning, but also such instances where the Latin future marker serves an `atemporal' function (see above). In addition to the true future meanings future and future progressive \citep[243]{hale_latin_1966}, the Latin future indicative can also have the following meanings: habitual action \citep[252]{hale_latin_1966}, the modal notion of command or prohibition \citep[304]{hale_latin_1966}, or the ``discovery of a state of affairs now existing" \citep[253]{hale_latin_1966}. 
We include all instances of morphologically marked future indicative, regardless of their more specific function. We look at the different functions separately in Section \ref{chap:context}.

    \textit{You \textbf{shall} not steal.} (Ex. 20:15)
    \item \textbf{condition}: protasis of a conditional:\\
    \textit{Or if he \textbf{asks} for a fish} $[\ldots]$
    \item \textbf{consequence}: the apodosis of a conditional:\\
     $[\ldots]$ \textit{\textbf{will give} him a serpent} (Mt. 7:10)
    \item \textbf{generic statement}: a statement not about a specific moment in the future but of general validity: \\
    \textit{whoever murders \textbf{will be liable} to judgment} (Mt. 5:21)
\end{itemize}

Besides the `future' meaning in the narrower sense, which we label \textit{prognosis} and which we subdivided into prognoses about singularly occurring events and such about habitually occurring events, we find the modal meaning of obligation or prohibition, which we labelled \textit{commandment} here. Much less frequent are sentences that are part of a condition-consequence structure, especially the conditions are rare. As these are naturally non-assertive, they are considered unspecific regarding temporal reference \citep[314]{Dahl2000}. Typologically only fully grammaticalised future markers (i.e. those that are obligatory) occur in these contexts \citep[314]{Dahl2000}.\footnote{In modern German the usage of \textit{werden} + infinitive is highly restricted in either part of conditional clauses \citep[]{vater1975}.}
Expressions of gnomic, general truth are (semantically) considered tenseless \citep[87]{Ultan1978}. They tend to also formally be unmarked for tense (i.e. languages often use the present tense for these) \citep[87]{Ultan1978}. However, some languages (e.g. Greek and English) also express it with future markers \citep[87]{Ultan1978}. In the Latin Vulgate, we do find sentences with future markers that express a gnomic truth, which could hint at this being possible in Latin, too. However, it could also be due to our operationalisation of ascribing gnomic truth. 

\section{Analyses}\label{chap:results}

\subsection{Distribution of \textit{wērden} + present participle and \textit{wērden} + infinitive}\label{chap:freq}
Before providing a deeper analysis on factors influencing the use of the different future constructions in Middle Low German, this section gives a first, rather rough glimpse of the frequencies of two of the constructions. The research literature suggests a connection between the development of \textit{wērden} + infinitive and \textit{wērden} + present participle. As our quantitative analysis in this section treats these constructions only, we can only draw conclusions about these patterns and not about future marking in general. The entire range of Middle Low German future constructions will be investigated in the next sections.

\begin{figure} %[h!]
   \includegraphics[scale=0.8]{ihden_schnee/freqren_halfcents.png}
  \caption{Diachronic distribution of \textit{wērden} + infinitive and \textit{wērden} + present participle in the ReN (normalised to corpus size, total counts given in labels)}
   \label{fig:ihden_schnee:freqren}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:ihden_schnee:freqren} shows the frequencies of \textit{wērden}~+ present participle (red) and \textit{wērden}~+ infinitive (blue) in the ReN.\footnote{All figures are created with ggplot \citep{wickham2016} in RStudio \citep{RCoreTeam2021}.}
The figure is based on the frequencies normalised to corpus size (n of finite verbs) with the raw frequencies given on top of each column. 
Before the 15\textsuperscript{th} century, attestations for both constructions are marginal, ranging from 0 to 4 and remaining far below one token per 1000 finite verbs in the corpus. Starting in the second half of the 15\textsuperscript{th} century, we find a sudden increase in attestations of both constructions, with \textit{wērden} + present participle being more frequent at first. In the following half century, where most of the attestations are found, the two constructions' frequencies are roughly the same, with \textit{wērden} + infinitive prevailing slightly. After that, we find, again, far fewer instances. For \textit{wērden} + present participle we find only one attestation in the last 150 years covered by the corpus. \textit{Wērden} + infinitive is also much less attested in the later period compared to the preceding one. However, as the corpus contains only a very small number of tokens from only three texts from this period, the relative frequency is still high. 

While the frequency development is consistent with the observations in the traditional Middle Low German grammars,\footnote{For example, \citet[222--223, § 412, note 6]{Lasch1914} states that the construction with the infinitive predominates over the one with present participle over time.} these results must be treated with caution. One caveat concerns the sparse data for the later periods of the corpus (the cessation of \textit{wērden} + present participle could be due to the small range of texts covered). Another one concerns the uneven distribution of future references in texts, as future references are rather rare and the occurrence of future markers of course depends on whether future events play a role in each specific text. 

The frequency peaks in Figure \ref{fig:ihden_schnee:freqren} can be attributed to some texts containing a large amount of future-related propositions: In the first half of the 16\textsuperscript{th} century, many of the instances (for both constructions) come from \textit{Der schapherders Kalender} (1523), a text containing a collection of prophecies for the different zodiac signs, which by nature of their content contain an exceptionally high number of future references. The second half of the 15\textsuperscript{th} and the whole 16\textsuperscript{th} century prominently feature Middle Low German Bible versions as well as other religious texts, which generally contain more references to the future than literary texts, for example. The majority of instances of \textit{wērden} + infinitive in the second half of the 17\textsuperscript{th} century can be attributed to the \textit{Veer Schertz=Gedichte}, a compilation of humorous poems by Lauremberg (1652). Since in the later times of Middle Low German the influence of High German increases, the high frequency of \textit{wērden}~+ infinitive in this text might be due to contact with High German where \textit{wērden}~+ infinitive is already the established construction at that time.

The varying likelihood of future references between individual texts but also between genres/text types limits the reliability of the frequencies found, given that the corpus is not balanced overall regarding the distribution of genre over time. For the 13\textsuperscript{th} and 14\textsuperscript{th} century, the corpus predominantly contains legal texts and deeds, as well as some texts of knowledge transfer. For the 15\textsuperscript{th} and 16\textsuperscript{th} century, however, religious texts and literature predominate and the 17\textsuperscript{th} century only features literary texts.\footnote{The distribution of tokens along the parameters of time, language area, and genre is visualised on the corpus website \href{https://www.slm.uni-hamburg.de/ren/korpus/korpusdesign.html}{https://www.slm.uni-hamburg.de/ren/korpus/korpusdesign.html} (last accessed on October 1 2023).} 
As mentioned above, the texts in which we find most instances of the \textit{wērden} constructions mainly belong to the fields of religious texts, literature and knowledge transfer (in the rather special case of the horoscopical \textit{Der schapherders Kalender}). Since these genres prevail in the later periods of the corpus, the results could possibly reflect this structure rather than a diachronic development in the usage of the constructions.  
We focus on one of the highly futurate text types found in the corpus, namely Bible translations, in the next section, while expanding the scope of our analysis with respect to the variants.

\subsubsection{The influence of the text and the translator}\label{chap:translator}

\largerpage[-0.5]

Having gained an overview of the various options for translating Latin future expressions into Middle Low German, we will now take a closer look at the choice of the various constructions in the three biblical texts. Examples (\ref{ex:ihden_schnee:1}) and (\ref{ex:ihden_schnee:2}) in the preceding section already indicate that the three Middle Low German translations can use different constructions for the same Latin future expression. The Latin \textit{habebit} `I will have' in Exodus 20:7 is translated by a) a present tense form (\textit{heft}) in the Bible of Halberstadt, b) \textit{wērden} + present participle (\textit{werd hebbende}) in the Bible of Lübeck, and c) \textit{schȫlen} + infinitive (\textit{schal entschuldighen}) in the Bible of Köln (see \ref{ex:ihden_schnee:1a}--\ref{ex:ihden_schnee:1c}). In Genesis 22:17, the Latin future \textit{benedicam} `I will bless' is translated by a) \textit{wērden} + infinitive (\textit{werde benedigen}) in the Bible of Halberstadt, whereas the Bible of Köln uses b) \textit{willen} + infinitive (\textit{wyl gebenedien}) (see \ref{ex:ihden_schnee:2a}--\ref{ex:ihden_schnee:2b}).

The observation that the choice of a future construction strongly depends on the text also shows in the frequencies presented in Figure \ref{fig:ihden_schnee:bible_ov} in the preceding section.  
The most crucial difference concerns the two constructions \textit{wērden} + infinitive and \textit{wērden} + present participle. While with 12\% in the Bible of Köln and 14\% in the Bible of Halberstadt \textit{wērden} + infinitive is an important option for translating Latin future, with 2\% in the Bible of Lübeck it is only marginally used. Instead, the Bible of Lübeck contains a relatively high amount of \textit{wērden} + present participle (22\%), which is used infrequently in the other two texts. As mentioned in Section \ref{chap:method}, the Bible of Lübeck is based on the Bible of Köln, and the Bible of Halberstadt is based on both of the others. Interestingly, the translators of the Bible of Lübeck and the Bible of Halberstadt make significant changes concerning the future expressions. They do not simply adapt the form from the base text – otherwise we would have similar frequencies for the different constructions in all three texts. In fact, the frequency of \textit{wērden} + infinitive and \textit{wērden} + present participle strikingly differs between the two Bibles from Köln and Halberstadt on the one hand and the Bible of Lübeck on the other hand.

\largerpage[-0.5]

As explained for the older varieties of High German in Section \ref{chap:lit} and as is suggested for Middle Low German in Section \ref{chap:freq}, \textit{wērden} + infinitive prevails over \textit{wērden} + present participle  diachronically. However, such a development is not reflected in the frequencies of these two constructions in the three Bibles -- there is no rise in the use of \textit{wērden} + infinitive from Köln via Lübeck to Halberstadt. Instead, the Bible of Köln and the Bible of Halberstadt show similar frequencies of the two constructions and the Bible of Lübeck stands out from the other two with a much higher proportion of \textit{wērden} + present participle and a much lower frequency of \textit{wērden} + infinitive. The frequent use of \textit{wērden} + present participle in the Bible of Lübeck is a choice of its translator and therefore a characteristic feature of this text. Whether the differences are solely due to the translator or whether an influence of the language area manifests itself in the translator's language cannot be clearly answered on the basis of the texts we have examined. The observation that Lübeck distinguishes itself from Köln and Halberstadt suggests a possible influence of the language area. From around 1370 to 1530, the language of Lübeck functioned as a supra-regional lingua franca \citep[1483]{Peters2000a}; it was regarded as the de facto Middle Low German standard. A higher proportion of \textit{wērden} + infinitive in Köln and Halberstadt could be explained by a stronger influence of High German, because Köln has a special status due to its location in the High German language area and Halberstadt is on the south-eastern edge of the Eastphalian area on the border to the Elbe Eastphalian area (see Figure \ref{fig:ihden_schnee:map} in Section \ref{chap:method}). The south of the Elbe Eastphalian area  already switched to Early New High German between 1350 and 1450 \citep[1417]{Peters2000b}; a certain influence of High German can also be assumed in the neighbouring areas.\footnote{Such an influence is confirmed by \citet{Czajkowski2021}, who examines Low German and High German forms on the phonological and graphematic level in the transitional area between Low German and East Central German. The locations of her corpus range from Sittichenbach in the south to Halberstadt and Aken in the north. The period examined extends from 1265 to 1490 \citep[31--36]{Czajkowski2021}. \citet[293--294]{Czajkowski2021} shows that the proportion of High German forms already increases from the second half of the 14\textsuperscript{th} century until the end of the 15\textsuperscript{th} century. It can be assumed that this language change manifested itself not only on the phonological and graphematic level but also in the syntax.}

Besides these differences concerning \textit{wērden} + present participle and \textit{wērden}~+ infinitive, the three texts share many similarities. In all three texts the present tense is the dominating structure for the translation of a Latin future expression, the frequencies between the Bibles only differ minimally. Moreover, all three texts make use of the modal verb construction with \textit{schȫlen} rather frequently.

Since the formally different options for expressing future in Middle Low German share the same basic semantics or the same grammatical function, they can easily be exchanged for each other within the same context as is illustrated by Tables \ref{tab:ihden_schnee:correspKLN} and \ref{tab:ihden_schnee:correspHBS} and by the examples analysed above. Hence, the use of one variant is clearly a choice of the translator. However, the reason why one translator prefers a variant against another one can hardly be discerned. One hypothesis might be that the translator uses one variant consciously in order to express a different semantic nuance of the sentence. Modal verb constructions could, for example, be preferred in order to convey the additional semantic nuance of modality. In order to verify our hypothesis, the following two sections take a closer look at the filling of the main verb slot of the future construction as well as at the semantic and syntactic context.

Most of the instances of \textit{wērden} in present tense translate a Latin future form of the verb \textit{esse} `be'. Instead of translating this future form into Middle Low German with the help of complex verbal constructions with the main verb \textit{wēsen} or \textit{sîn}\footnote{Middle Low German has two different verbs meaning ‘to be’, namely, \textit{wēsen} and \textit{sîn}.}~-- the equivalent of Latin \textit{esse} --, for example \textit{schal sîn} or \textit{wērt sîn}, the three texts prefer the present tense of \textit{wērden}. This choice of construction is only possible because of the semantic connection between \textit{wērden} in present tense and \textit{wēsen} in future tense, as in \textit{De sunde wert iegenwardich} 'Sin becomes present' and \textit{De sunde wert sin iegenwardich} 'Sin will be present'. If \textit{X} (here: the sin) becomes \textit{Y} (here: present) today it means that \textit{X} will be \textit{Y} tomorrow.

This analysis showed a clear impact of verb semantics on the choice of future construction in the case of \textit{wērden}. This effect can be accounted for by the verb's semantics, as it denotes a change of state and thus already brings about a future reading when used in the present. An additional marking of futurity is unnecessary in such cases. The occurrence of the \textit{wērden} + infinitive construction with \textit{wērden} is considered to indicate an advanced level of grammaticalisation of the construction and is found from the 16\textsuperscript{th} century on in High German (\cite[17]{Schmid2000}; \cite[28]{concu_werden_2022}). It indicates an expansion of the contexts of occurrence and it shows the gradual bleaching of meaning in the auxiliary, which leads to a differentiation from its main verb usage and thus an ease of potential blocking effects to use both words in the same predicate. It is in line with previous research as well as our findings from Section \ref{chap:freq} that we do not find such signs of grammaticalisation in the Middle Low German Bibles.\footnote{We find but 12 instances of \textit{wērden} + \textit{wērden} in the ReN. Most of them are instantiations of an embedded  passive construction (\textit{wērden} + \textit{wērden} + past participle). Nine of them are from the first half of the 16\textsuperscript{th} century, the rest from later periods.}

The role of sentence semantics, which has already been indicated in the preceding analysis, is examined more closely in the following section in order to identify potential correlations.

While there are different combinations, there is a pattern within these sentences, namely the preference of the different texts for certain constructions, as was discussed in Section \ref{chap:bibles_freq}: We see a prevalence of the \textit{schȫlen} construction in Köln, \textit{wērden} + present participle in Lübeck and \textit{wērden} + infinitive in Halberstadt, which is illustrated in (\ref{ex:ihden_schnee:Gen22:8KLN}--\ref{ex:ihden_schnee:Gen22:8HBS}).

Let us return briefly to the construction \textit{schȫlen} + infinitive. In example (\ref{ex:ihden_schnee:Gen22:8}) we saw it used in a prognostic (i.e. `real future') utterance in the Bible of Köln, which we have found to show a preference of this construction. In (\ref{ex:ihden_schnee:Mt6:31}) it is the Bible of Lübeck that uses \textit{schȫlen} while the other two Bibles have present forms. (\ref{ex:ihden_schnee:Mt7:21}) shows the only non-directive sentence where Halberstadt uses the construction (alongside both other texts) and it might be up to debate in this case whether the sentence does or does not bear a modal meaning. Overall, regarding the different usages of  \textit{schȫlen} + infinitive, the situation is as follows: As was shown in Section \ref{chap:bibles_freq}, the construction appears most frequent in Köln and less frequent in Lübeck and Halberstadt (see Figure \ref{fig:ihden_schnee:bible_ov}). The proportion of non-directive (prognosis, habitual and generic) usage, however, is comparable in Lübeck and Köln, with about a third of the instances in Lübeck and more than a fourth in Köln. In Halberstadt, however, there are no non-directive instances of \textit{schȫlen}~+ infinitive except the one shown in (\ref{ex:ihden_schnee:Mt7:21a}). Looking back at the frequency distribution found for the constructions with \textit{wērden}, we could tentatively suggest a decline of \textit{schȫlen} + infinitive as a future construction, given that, in the period where we see an increase in the \textit{wērden} constructions, we find no more usage of \textit{schȫlen}~+ infinitive in sentences that are futurate in the narrow sense (i.e. excluding directiva) in the Bible translation. This conclusion is of course tentative and is in urgent need of verification on the basis of larger amounts of data.

This analysis showed that different types of sentence semantics clearly have an effect on the choice of the construction for translating the Latin future. We find a strong preference for the modal construction with \textit{schȫlen} and the imperative for directive statements like commandments and interdictions. Both parts of conditional clauses clearly prefer the present construction, as do generic statements, albeit with a little more variation. The sentence types that seem to be the least determined with regard to the construction are prognoses, both about singular and habitual events. The latter are the sentences where most of the \textit{wērden}~+ infinitive are found. Overall, the results regarding the `extra' categories considered here, on top of the `real future' categories, are inline with what would be expected based on the literature. We want to underline, however, that these results do contribute to an understanding of future reference in Middle Low German and that using a broader definition of future, encompassing constructions that overlap semantically, showed to be fruitful for an initial and exploratory study like the present one. 

\section{Summary and Outlook}\label{chap:summary}
Our empirical investigation of future constructions and the translation of Latin
future forms in Middle Low German has brought the following results to light:
In the first part of the study, we conducted a purely quantitative examination of
the occurrence of the two competing constructions \textit{wērden} + present participle
and \textit{wērden} + infinitive. Our data, taken from the ReN, show an increase in the occurrence of both variants in the second half of the 15\textsuperscript{th} century and the first half of the 16\textsuperscript{th} century. It is not possible to draw conclusions about the development of the constructions prior to the 15\textsuperscript{th} century on the basis of the structure of the corpus, as texts that contain more references to the future are only represented from the 15\textsuperscript{th} century on. A number of differences can still be noted in relation to their development in High German. Firstly, the development differs strikingly from that observed in High German in that \textit{wērden} + infinitive does not seem to replace \textit{wērden} + present participle over time. Instead, we see a rather sudden increase in both constructions. Furthermore, the frequency growth of \textit{wērden} + present participle in Middle Low German takes place at a time when it no longer plays a central role in High German. In our data, we cannot trace its demise. Its absence in the 17\textsuperscript{th} century should not be overestimated because of the few texts contained in that time span of the corpus. Overall, it must be noted that the suitability of reference corpora for the investigation of phenomena that are dependent on the content of the texts, such as future reference, is not necessarily given and must be checked and evaluated. 

In the second part of our study, we undertook a quantitative-qualitative investigation of the translation of Latin future forms in three Middle Low German Bibles. The benefit of this analysis is its onomasiological approach: Starting from the function, namely expressing the future of an event, which in Latin is morphologically marked on the verb, we examined all the formally different constructions sharing this function (i.e. also the present tense and the modal constructions). Due to their multi-functionality, in a corpus linguistic approach, for example, finding attestations for these is rather difficult or requires a great deal of effort. In our analysis, we identified a set of intertwined factors influencing  the choice of variant. One of them could be the translator, more precisely their preference for specific structures or their individual reading of the Latin original. However, the language area could also be a decisive factor, since Köln and Halberstadt are more susceptible to the influence of High German than Lübeck. With regard to constructions expressing future, the three texts differ above all in that the proportion of \textit{wērden} + present participle is considerably higher in the Bible of Lübeck than in the Bible of Köln and the Bible of Halberstadt.

Regarding the main verbs in the Middle Low German future construction our examination revealed a connection with the type of construction for the verb \textit{wērden}, that is, it occurs predominantly in present tense. As the change of state semantics of the verb itself can already bring about a future reading, an additional marking of futurity is not necessary. The fact that \textit{wērden} does not occur in the verb slot of \textit{wērden} + infinitive shows that the construction has not reached the same level of grammaticalisation in Middle Low German that it has in Early New High German, where \textit{werden} + \textit{werden} occurs from the 16\textsuperscript{th} century on.

The semantic context also has an influence on the choice of variant. Here, a strong connection between commandments and the use of \textit{schȫlen} + infinitive was found. This in turn is also reflected in the fact that \textit{schȫlen} + infinitive is predominantly used in the second person singular. For this reason, the Ten Commandments of the Bible contain a particularly large number of verbal complexes with \textit{schȫlen}. We were also able to show that the present tense variant occurs mainly in conditions and consequences.

% Our study has shown that only the combination of quantitative and qualitative analysis of corpus data can yield reliable results on the occurrence of formally different constructions used for referring to future events in Middle Low German as well as on the influence of intra- and extra-linguistic factors on the choice of variant.
In order to gain an even more comprehensive insight into the use of future expressions in Middle Low German, it would be helpful to look at other text types besides the Bible texts studied here, such as the calendar mentioned above. However, it should be borne in mind that manual annotation of all future tense expressions in such a text is very time-consuming and unambiguous annotation is not always possible, especially in the case of modal verb constructions. Despite extensive corpora, the study of the future in German remains a complex and challenging task.
